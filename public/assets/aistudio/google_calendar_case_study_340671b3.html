Case study brief

The opportunity

Google Calendar is already effective at helping people manage events and find available time. But for distributed teams, scheduling is not simply a matter of finding an empty slot. A time can be technically available while still being inconvenient for one or more participants. After a meeting, the information created during the discussion can also become difficult to rediscover.

This case study explores how Calendar could become a stronger collaboration layer around two moments in the meeting lifecycle: coordinating a meeting before it happens and retaining useful context after it ends.

Opportunity 1: Collaborative scheduling

Help organisers and participants converge on a mutually acceptable time across time zones, working hours and competing preferences.

Opportunity 2: Contextual meeting history

Make decisions, action items and meeting notes easier to find from the Calendar event where the meeting happened.

Core product thesis

Google Calendar could evolve from a tool that primarily manages people's schedules into a more complete collaboration platform that helps teams coordinate meetings before they happen and retain context after they end.

The two opportunities are related, but they are not equally urgent. Collaborative scheduling is the primary MVP opportunity. Meeting continuity is a follow-on capability that builds on Calendar's existing connection with Google Meet and Google Drive.

01 · Product understanding

Product Overview

What is Google Calendar?

Google Calendar is a digital calendar and scheduling product that helps individuals and teams organise events, coordinate availability, invite participants and manage their time. It is available to personal users and as part of Google Workspace, where it connects with products such as Gmail, Google Meet and Google Drive.

Its core value is not just storing appointments. It provides a shared representation of time: users can see commitments, understand availability, invite others and coordinate around events. For teams, this makes Calendar part of the collaboration infrastructure.

Strategic opportunity

Calendar sits at the point where people coordinate their time. That gives it a natural role in helping teams move from scheduling a meeting to preparing for it, and then preserving what came out of it. The opportunity is to reduce coordination friction without turning Calendar into a full project-management or document-authoring product.

No primary user interviews or surveys have been conducted for this draft. The user problems below are therefore opportunities to validate, not claims that every Calendar user experiences the same friction.

Published research: time-zone collaboration

A 2023 CHI study, Challenging but Connective: Large-Scale Characteristics of Synchronous Collaboration Across Time Zones, analysed 20 million meetings scheduled at a multinational corporation and was supported by a survey of 130 employees. The researchers found that cross-time-zone meetings were associated with early-morning and late-evening scheduling patterns that often conflicted with employees' stated temporal preferences. They also found that the burden was distributed unevenly across organisational levels and geographies.[5]

The study also cautions against a simplistic conclusion that cross-time-zone meetings should simply be reduced. Such meetings can connect people who are organisationally distant and diverse. The product opportunity is therefore not to eliminate inconvenient meetings, but to help teams make better-informed and fairer trade-offs when synchronous collaboration is necessary.

Research implication

Availability is not the same as convenience. A scheduling product should help teams understand who is absorbing the inconvenience, not only identify an empty slot on a calendar.

03 · Frame the opportunity

Problem Identification

Problem A: Finding an available time is not the same as finding an acceptable time

For a distributed team, the calendar grid can reveal when people are free, but it does not necessarily reveal which of several available times is least disruptive to the group. One participant may have an early start, another may be at the end of their working day, and a third may have limited flexibility because of personal or operational commitments.

When there is no clear way to collect preferences, organisers may rely on repeated messages, informal negotiation or their own judgement. Participants may accept a slot even when it is inconvenient. Over time, the same people can end up absorbing a disproportionate share of early or late meetings.

Problem statement A

Meeting organisers coordinating across time zones need a lightweight way to compare candidate times against participants' real preferences, because availability alone does not show whether a meeting time is mutually acceptable or distributes inconvenience fairly.

Underlying causes to investigate

  • Availability data represents calendar commitments, not the full range of personal preferences or constraints.
  • Time-zone differences make it harder to compare the impact of a proposed time across participants.
  • Participants may be reluctant to suggest alternatives, especially when the organiser is senior or the meeting is important.
  • Existing scheduling suggestions can identify candidate slots, but the group may still need to express preferences and make a collective decision.
  • Repeated meetings can create an uneven distribution of inconvenient time slots if the burden is not visible.

Problem B: Meeting context becomes difficult to rediscover

A meeting can produce decisions, action items, follow-ups and useful context. But when someone misses the meeting or needs to revisit a decision weeks later, they may have to search through Calendar events, Drive documents, notes and chat messages. The event title may not contain the specific topic they remember, and the notes may be stored separately from the event.

Gemini-generated meeting notes can already be associated with Calendar events for eligible users. The opportunity is to make that context easier to scan and retrieve from the Calendar experience, while keeping the original notes as the source of truth.

Problem statement B

People who need to revisit a past meeting need a fast way to identify its key decisions and locate the original notes, because meeting context can become fragmented across events and documents.

Opportunity statement

How might Google Calendar help distributed teams reach a mutually acceptable meeting time with less coordination effort, while making the outcomes of those meetings easier to find later?

The first problem is prioritised because it occurs before every meeting and directly affects whether collaboration can happen. The second problem is valuable, but it depends on the quality, availability and permissions of meeting notes and can be developed as a follow-on experience.

05 · Design the intervention

Proposed Solutions

Solution A: Collaborative Time Slot Selection

Proposed concept Add a structured multi-option scheduling poll to the Calendar event-creation workflow. The goal is to help participants express preferences and help organisers make a decision without managing a long chain of messages.

How the experience works

  1. Create the event: The organiser enters the meeting title, duration, attendees and relevant context.
  2. Suggest candidate slots: Gemini uses information the organiser is authorised to access, such as availability, time zones, working hours and existing conflicts, to generate a shortlist. This builds on existing Gemini scheduling suggestions.
  3. Select multiple options: The organiser reviews the shortlist, adjusts it if needed and selects several candidate times for a poll.
  4. Set the response deadline: The organiser chooses when voting closes. Participants can see the deadline and whether the poll is still open.
  5. Display local times: Each participant sees candidate slots in their own local time zone. The organiser can also inspect the corresponding time in participants' zones.
  6. Collect preferences: Participants select the slots that work for them. The interface distinguishes a preferred time from a time they can attend if necessary.
  7. Offer a private alternative: A participant can suggest another time. This suggestion is visible only to the organiser by default, reducing the social pressure of publicly challenging a proposed slot.
  8. Review results: The organiser sees aggregate availability and preferences, including conflicts and alternative suggestions, and can assess the trade-offs.
  9. Confirm: The organiser selects the final time. Calendar updates the event and notifies invitees.

Key design decisions

Important interaction choices and rationale

Longer-term extension: Fairer recurring meetings

For recurring meetings across time zones, Calendar could optionally show how early and late meeting times have been distributed over time. It could suggest a rotation when the same participants repeatedly absorb inconvenient hours. The organiser would remain in control, and the feature would need to account for local working hours, holidays and individual preferences.

This should not be part of the first release. A fairness score could oversimplify personal circumstances, and the product should not imply that all inconvenient meeting times are equally burdensome.

Solution B: Contextual Meeting History

Proposed concept Make the Calendar event a useful entry point for revisiting what happened in a meeting. The proposal builds on existing Gemini meeting notes in Meet and improves how the resulting context is surfaced.

How the experience works

  1. Gemini-generated meeting notes are associated with the relevant Calendar event, where the feature is enabled and available.
  2. The event displays a concise summary card with key discussion points, decisions, action items and follow-ups.
  3. Each summary section is clearly labelled as AI-generated and includes a link to the original Google Doc.
  4. Users can navigate a chronological history of past meetings and open the relevant event.
  5. As a later extension, users can search meeting history using natural language, for example: “Find the meeting where we discussed the API migration.”

Design principles

  • Preserve source access: The original notes document remains accessible. The summary is a shortcut, not a replacement for the source.
  • Respect permissions: The summary must inherit or enforce the access rules of the underlying meeting and document. A Calendar attendee should not automatically gain access to restricted notes.
  • Make uncertainty visible: AI-generated summaries should not present inferred statements as confirmed decisions.
  • Support correction: Users should be able to open the source and correct or report an inaccurate summary.
  • Keep the interface lightweight: Calendar should surface the context people need, not become a full document editor.

How the proposals fit together

Collaborative scheduling improves the process before a meeting. Contextual meeting history improves the process after it. Together, they strengthen Calendar's role as a collaboration hub while keeping its central job clear: helping people coordinate time and commitments.

06 · Decide what to build first

Prioritisation and MVP

Prioritisation principle

The first release should solve one coherent problem end to end. Building scheduling polls, AI meeting summaries, historical search and recurring-meeting fairness at the same time would create a broad scope with multiple dependencies. The MVP should focus on helping a group select and confirm a mutually acceptable time.

RICE framework

RICE is a prioritisation framework that compares candidate initiatives using estimated reach, impact, confidence and effort. It is useful for structuring a discussion, but reliable internal usage and engineering estimates are not available for this independent analysis. I therefore use qualitative prioritisation rather than inventing numerical scores.

RICE =Reach × Impact × ConfidenceEffortQualitative assessment only in this case study

Reach estimates how many users or workflows are affected. Impact estimates the size of the improvement. Confidence reflects the strength of the evidence. Effort represents the product, design, engineering and operational work required.

MVP scope

The MVP is a collaborative scheduling flow that begins with a Calendar event and ends with a confirmed meeting.

  • Organiser creates an event and adds participants.
  • Calendar suggests candidate slots using authorised availability and time-zone information.
  • Organiser selects multiple candidate slots and sets a deadline.
  • Participants view options in local time, indicate preferred and possible slots, and can privately suggest an alternative.
  • Organiser reviews aggregate results and confirms the final slot.
  • Calendar updates the event and sends the normal event notifications.

What should not be built in the MVP

  • Automatic booking based only on the highest vote count. The organiser should retain final confirmation.
  • Public display of individual votes. This could create social pressure and expose personal constraints.
  • Complex fairness scoring or automated recurring-meeting rotation before the product understands user preferences.
  • A broad meeting-management workspace that duplicates project-management or document-authoring tools.
  • Natural-language search across all historical meeting content before permission handling and retrieval quality are proven.

Why this sequence?

Scheduling is a frequent, bounded workflow with a clear completion event: the meeting is confirmed. It can be tested without requiring every meeting to use AI-generated notes. Meeting continuity is a logical next step, but it introduces additional dependencies around note availability, summary accuracy and document permissions. Sequencing the work reduces scope and makes it easier to learn whether the first intervention creates value.

07 · Define success

Measuring Success

North Star metric

Share of cross-time-zone meeting requests confirmed by the deadline while respecting participants' stated working-hour preferences.

This is a proposed outcome metric, not a metric currently reported by Google. It focuses on successful coordination without optimising for the raw number of meetings created.

Guardrail metrics

  • Poll abandonment: A faster workflow is not useful if participants do not complete it.
  • Meeting cancellations and rescheduling: A confirmed slot should not create more downstream disruption.
  • Preference concentration: Check whether the same people continue to receive inconvenient meeting times.
  • Notification volume: Poll reminders should not create notification fatigue.
  • Summary correction rate: Frequent corrections may indicate inaccurate or misleading AI summaries.
  • Permission incidents: No summary or historical search result should expose notes to unauthorised users.
  • Meeting load: Do not optimise the feature in a way that encourages more meetings without improving collaboration outcomes.

08 · Learn before scaling

Experimentation and Validation

Research before implementation

Before committing to a full build, I would conduct approximately 5 to 8 exploratory interviews with people who regularly coordinate work across time zones. This is a small qualitative sample intended to reveal workflow patterns and language, not to produce statistically representative findings.

Key questions would include:

  • Walk me through the last time you scheduled a meeting with people in different time zones.
  • How did you choose the candidate times?
  • How did participants communicate that a proposed time was inconvenient?
  • Who tends to adjust their schedule when no time works well for everyone?
  • Do recurring meetings rotate across time zones, or do the same people usually adjust?
  • What happens when someone misses a meeting and needs to understand the decision?
  • Where do you look today for meeting notes, decisions and action items?

Experiment 1: Does a collaborative poll reduce scheduling friction?

Hypothesis: For meetings with several participants across time zones, a structured multi-option poll will reduce time to confirmation and manual follow-up compared with the existing scheduling workflow, without reducing participant satisfaction.

Proposed experiment design
ElementPlan
Target usersOrganisers scheduling meetings with multiple participants across at least two time zones.
ControlExisting Calendar scheduling workflow, including current availability and time suggestions where available.
TreatmentCollaborative poll with multiple candidate slots, local-time display, preference collection, private alternatives and a response deadline.

Decision ruleContinue if the treatment improves confirmation efficiency without worsening participant satisfaction or concentrating inconvenient times on a subset of users.

Experiment 2: Should aggregate poll results be visible before voting?

Showing aggregate results can help participants understand the group's preferences, but it can also create majority bias. People may change their vote to align with the apparent majority or avoid selecting a time that looks unpopular.

Proposed design comparison
Variant AVariant BWhat to learn
Participants submit preferences without seeing other responses. Aggregate results appear after submission.Participants can see aggregate results while choosing their preferences.Does early visibility improve decision quality, or does it create conformity and reduce honest preference reporting?

Compare preference changes, completion rate, time to decision and participant-reported comfort. Individual votes should remain private by default in both variants.

Experiment 3: Do contextual summaries improve information retrieval?

Hypothesis: Showing key decisions and action items inside the Calendar event will reduce the time users need to retrieve information from a past meeting, compared with opening the notes document and searching manually.

  • Run moderated usability sessions with representative tasks, such as finding a decision from a meeting several weeks earlier.
  • Compare a current workflow with a prototype that includes an inline summary and a link to the original notes.
  • Measure task completion, time to find the correct information, incorrect answers and user confidence.
  • Include cases where notes are unavailable, incomplete or restricted by permissions.
  • Do not launch natural-language search until retrieval quality and permission enforcement have been evaluated.

Rollout approach

  1. Discovery: Conduct interviews and map current scheduling workflows.
  2. Prototype: Test the poll flow, local-time display and privacy controls before engineering investment.
  3. Limited pilot: Release to a narrow eligible cohort and monitor primary and guardrail metrics.
  4. Iteration: Improve poll completion, preference collection and deadline handling based on observed friction.
  5. Expansion: Broaden availability only after the workflow demonstrates value across different team sizes and time-zone patterns.

Key trade-offs

  • Speed versus inclusion: Automatically selecting the most popular slot may be fast, but it can overlook a participant with a less common time-zone constraint. The MVP should support a fast decision while keeping the trade-offs visible to the organiser.
  • Transparency versus independence: Showing poll results can make group preferences visible, but may influence individual votes. This should be tested rather than assumed to be beneficial.
  • Automation versus control: Gemini can reduce the effort of finding options, but the organiser should retain control over the final decision.
  • Convenience versus privacy: Summaries make meeting context easier to access, but must not weaken the permissions of the original notes.
  • Personalisation versus complexity: Fairness recommendations may help recurring teams, but a complex fairness model could be difficult to explain and may not reflect individual circumstances.

What I would validate next

  1. Whether distributed teams experience enough coordination friction to justify a dedicated poll workflow.
  2. Whether participants prefer selecting a single best time, multiple acceptable times or ranking their options.
  3. Whether private alternative suggestions make it easier to communicate constraints.
  4. Whether aggregate poll results should be visible before or after a participant votes.
  5. How teams define an inconvenient meeting time and whether they want recurring meetings to rotate.
  6. Whether inline summaries measurably improve information retrieval beyond the existing link to Gemini-generated notes.

Reflection

The central insight from this analysis is that scheduling is not purely a calendar-availability problem. It is a coordination and decision-making problem involving preferences, power dynamics, time zones and trade-offs. A useful product should not simply find an empty slot faster. It should help people understand the impact of the options and make a decision that participants can accept.

The same principle applies after the meeting. Generating more notes is not enough if people cannot find, trust or act on the information later. The value comes from connecting meeting context to the moment when someone needs it, while preserving access controls and the original source.

Final product takeaway

Better collaboration is not achieved by adding more meetings or more AI features. It comes from reducing the friction around meaningful coordination, making trade-offs visible and helping teams carry decisions forward.
